Florida: Title II PDF backlog on special district websites

Data · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources linked inline

We found 26 working special-district websites (transit, water, fire, park, library and similar districts) on .gov domains registered in Florida in CISA's public .gov registry. Special districts are Title II public entities; under the ADA Title II web rule (28 CFR part 35, subpart H) special district governments have until April 26, 2028 to make web content, PDFs included, conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA, subject to the rule's exceptions.

Does ADA Title II apply to Florida?

Yes. Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act is federal law and covers every state and local government in every state: Florida's state agencies and public universities, counties, cities, towns, school districts and special districts. The 2024 web rule adds technical requirements for their websites, apps and the documents they post (WCAG 2.1 Level AA). Its compliance date depends on population: April 26, 2027 for governments serving 50,000 or more people (every state government included), April 26, 2028 for smaller ones and for special districts. This is general information, not legal advice.

We measured the PDFs these sites link from their pages. This page summarizes Florida's special-district sites as a whole. It does not name or rank individual districts. Districts without a registered .gov domain are not included. For cities and counties, see the city and county editions.

Florida at a glance

  • 16 of 26 special-district sites measured with a usable sample (20 or more PDFs). The rest blocked automated visitors, host documents on other systems, or had too few PDFs to sample; that is not a clean result.
  • 860 PDFs analyzed. 44.5% had no accessibility tags, higher than the national figure of 40.0%. 15.6% were scanned images with no usable text, and 46.3% did not declare a document language.
  • 0 of 48 sampled PDFs passed the veraPDF PDF/UA-1 checks.
  • The median measured site has roughly 385 pages of PDFs failing at least one basic check; across the measured sites that is on the order of 55,000 pages.
  • 53% of the sampled PDFs were modified in 2024 or later. Recent documents are more likely to still be in active use, and documents used to apply for, access or participate in a service are not covered by the rule's exception for preexisting documents.

What this means for Florida special districts

An untagged PDF gives a screen reader no headings, no reading order and no table structure. A scanned PDF gives it nothing at all. Both typically fail WCAG 2.1 AA (for example 1.3.1 Info and Relationships, and for scans 1.1.1 Non-text Content), and both are common in board agendas, budgets, rate notices and forms.

Not every old PDF has to be fixed. The rule exempts archived web content and preexisting conventional electronic documents unless they are currently used to apply for, access or participate in a service. The practical order is usually: fix the export settings so new documents are born accessible, remediate forms and anything people use to get a service, and move true archives to a clearly marked archive section. What this is, and what it isn't

These are automated signals, not a conformance evaluation. An automated check can show that a PDF fails; it cannot show that a PDF conforms. Estimates are scaled from samples, and a few very long documents can move a number a lot. The full method is on the national Backlog Index page.
