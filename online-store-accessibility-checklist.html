Online store accessibility checklist: test your shop in an hour

Accessibility complaints and demand letters aimed at online stores usually cite the same handful of problems: shoppers using a keyboard or a screen reader can't get through the menu, product options, cart or checkout. The fixes are mostly ordinary theme and content changes. This checklist covers what to test first, using only your keyboard and your browser.

General information, not legal advice. The usual reference standard is WCAG 2.2 Level AA.

1. Keyboard only (15 minutes)

Put the mouse away. Press Tab to move forward, Shift+Tab to go back, Enter and Space to activate. Try to buy a product.

  • Can you open the main menu and reach every link in it?
  • Can you pick size and color, change quantity, and add to cart?
  • Can you open and close the cart drawer, pop-ups and the cookie banner, and does Escape close them?
  • Can you always see where you are? A visible focus outline is required (WCAG 2.4.7). Many themes remove it.
  • Do you ever get stuck inside something (a carousel, a chat widget, a video)?

2. Images and product media (10 minutes)

  • Every product image has alt text that says what a shopper needs to know: “Navy wool beanie, folded cuff, front view”, not the file name or “image”.
  • Text inside images (sale banners, size charts) is also available as real text.
  • Videos have captions; nothing autoplays with sound.

3. Forms and checkout (10 minutes)

  • Every field has a visible label, not only placeholder text that disappears.
  • Error messages say what is wrong in words (“Enter a 5-digit ZIP code”), not just a red border.
  • Required fields are marked in text, not only by color.
  • Discount-code, newsletter and search fields are labeled too.

4. Color, text and zoom (10 minutes)

  • Text contrast is at least 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text). Light gray on white and text on photos are the usual failures.
  • Prices, sale badges and stock status don't rely on color alone.
  • Zoom the browser to 200%: nothing gets cut off or overlaps, and you don't have to scroll sideways.
  • Buttons and links are big enough to tap (WCAG 2.2 asks for at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels).

5. Structure (10 minutes)

  • Each page has one main heading (the product or collection name) and sensible subheadings.
  • Link and button names make sense on their own: “Add Navy Beanie to cart”, not a bare icon.
  • The page title in the browser tab is specific to the page.
  • PDF size charts, care guides and policies are tagged PDFs. Check one with the free PDF quick check.

About overlay widgets

One-line “accessibility widgets” that add a toolbar to your site don't change the underlying theme code, so they don't fix missing labels, keyboard traps or missing alt text. Disability advocates widely criticize them, and they don't stop complaints. Fix the theme and content instead; most fixes are one-time.
