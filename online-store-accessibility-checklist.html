Online store accessibility checklist: test your shop in an hour

Checklist · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources linked inline

Start by checking whether a shopper can navigate, choose options, add a product and complete checkout using a keyboard and assistive technology. This checklist covers what to test first, using only your keyboard and your browser. It is a preliminary review, not a complete conformance assessment.

General information, not legal advice. This checklist uses WCAG 2.2 Level AA as a technical reference; it does not determine which laws or contractual requirements apply to your store.

1. Keyboard only (15 minutes)

Put the mouse away. Press Tab to move forward, Shift+Tab to go back, Enter and Space to activate. Try to buy a product.

  • Can you open the main menu and reach every link in it?
  • Can you pick size and color, change quantity, and add to cart?
  • Can you open and close the cart drawer, pop-ups and the cookie banner with the keyboard? (A reachable close button matters most; Escape closing dialogs is a helpful convention.)
  • Can you always see where you are? WCAG 2.4.7 requires a visible keyboard focus indicator (it doesn't have to be an outline).
  • Do you ever get stuck inside something (a carousel, a chat widget, a video)?

2. Images and product media (10 minutes)

  • Informative images have useful text alternatives that say what a shopper needs to know: “Navy wool beanie, folded cuff, front view”, not the file name or “image”. Decorative or redundant images should be ignored by assistive technology, and linked images need a name that describes the link.
  • Text inside images (sale banners, size charts) is also available as real text.
  • Videos have captions. Recommended: nothing autoplays with sound. (WCAG 1.4.2 specifically requires a way to pause, stop or control audio that plays automatically for more than three seconds.)

3. Forms and checkout (10 minutes)

  • Every field has a visible label, not only placeholder text that disappears.
  • Error messages say what is wrong in words (“Enter a 5-digit ZIP code”), not just a red border.
  • Required fields are marked in text, not only by color.
  • Discount-code, newsletter and search fields are labeled too.

4. Color, text and zoom (10 minutes)

  • Text contrast is at least 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text). Light gray on white and text on photos are the usual failures.
  • Prices, sale badges and stock status don't rely on color alone.
  • At 200% text size, nothing is lost or stops working. Separately, test reflow at a 320 CSS-pixel-wide window (about 400% zoom on a 1280-pixel screen): content shouldn't need sideways scrolling, except things that genuinely need two dimensions, like large tables or maps.
  • Buttons and controls are big enough to tap: WCAG 2.2 generally asks for 24 by 24 CSS pixels, or enough spacing, with exceptions such as links inside a sentence.

5. Structure (10 minutes)

  • Recommended: each page has one main heading (the product or collection name) and sensible subheadings.
  • Link and button names make sense on their own: “Add Navy Beanie to cart”, not a bare icon.
  • The page title in the browser tab is specific to the page.
  • PDF size charts, care guides and policies are tagged PDFs. Check one with the free PDF quick check.

About overlay widgets

Don't treat installing a widget as proof of accessibility. Test the actual shopping journey and fix the barriers you find.

What an audit adds
