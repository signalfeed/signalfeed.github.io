Checking a PDF remediation quote: a worked example

How-to · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources linked inline

A remediation quote is only as good as the page count under it. This worked example uses a made-up public entity to show how much the answer moves with the price per page and with what you decide is in scope. The numbers are illustrative, not measurements of any real government.

The example entity

  • 1,200 PDFs on its website (agendas, minutes, budgets, plans, forms).
  • About 8 pages per PDF on average, so roughly 9,600 pages.
  • After an inventory, about 40% turn out to be archived or old documents nobody uses to apply for or access a service, which the ADA Title II rule's exceptions may cover. That leaves about 5,760 pages in scope. (Whether a document qualifies is a judgment for your own staff or counsel.)

The spread between the cheapest and dearest quote is five times, and counting what's in scope before signing cuts every column by 40%. Public contract prices for ordinary pages cluster around $3.50 to $6 per page, with complex, form and scanned pages costing more (sources and percentiles).

Questions to ask before you sign

  • Is the price per page, per document or a flat fee? Per-document prices hide long files; flat fees hide the page count the vendor assumed. Ask for that assumption in writing.
  • What's included? Alt text for charts and maps, table headers, fillable forms, OCR for scanned pages, and rush turnaround are often extra.
  • How is the work checked? Automated checks (veraPDF, Acrobat) catch structure problems; they don't prove a screen reader can use the file. Ask what manual review is done.
  • Is it a subscription? Software that fixes documents as they're posted is priced per month or year; compare it against the one-time cost of the backlog plus fixing new documents at the source (in Word).
  • What happens to new documents? If staff keep posting untagged PDFs, the backlog grows back. Accessible Word templates prevent most of it.
